Vegas SORRankingsMethodology

How Vegas SOR works

One reference team plays every schedule. The ranking measures how often it would finish with at least as many wins as the real team earned.

1. Read the closing spreads

For each completed 2026 FBS regular-season game, we collect the result, site and archived home-team closing spread from ESPN. A home favorite of seven becomes an expected home margin of +7. DraftKings has priority when multiple books are available. Games against FCS teams count. If a closing spread is unavailable, the result still counts toward the record and the rating fit uses the other available games.

2. Infer team strengths in points

The market quotes a line for the two teams that actually played. To estimate what a common reference team would do, we fit all the lines together. Each game's projected home margin equals the home team's rating minus the visiting team's rating, plus a home-field adjustment. A neutral site gets zero adjustment.

Weighted least squares minimizes squared errors between those projections and the observed lines. A line from 35 days ago gets half the weight of the latest line. The fit shrinks FBS ratings toward zero with a penalty weight of 0.10 and FCS team deviations toward a shared FCS rating with weight 2. The FCS baseline has weight 0.001 toward zero. Home field has weight 1 toward 2.5 points and is re-estimated each week. These choices stabilize early weeks when teams have few common opponents. Actual victory margins do not enter the fit.

3. Put the reference team on the schedule

The reference rating is the arithmetic mean of the 25 highest-rated FBS teams in that week's market fit. For each opponent we subtract its rating from the reference rating, then add home field, subtract it on the road, or leave the difference unchanged at a neutral site. Every team faces the same benchmark.

p(win) = 1 / (1 + exp(−0.11295 × expected margin))

The conversion was fitted to 3,945 college games with a recorded spread and final score from 2021–25 in The Prediction Tracker. At a projected margin of zero, the reference team wins 50% of the time. A seven-point advantage gives about 69%. These probabilities estimate hypothetical matchups.

4. Count every path to the record

Start with a 100% chance of zero wins. For each game with win chance p, multiply each current win-count probability by 1 − p to stay at the same count, and by p to move up one. After the last game, add the probabilities at or above the team's actual win count. This computes the Poisson-binomial tail exactly under the assumption of independent game outcomes given the ratings.

For a 7–2 team, all paths to seven, eight or nine wins count. A 10% chance earns a higher rank than a 30% chance. Identical schedules and records earn identical scores even if the teams won different games. Teams with zero wins have a 100% chance of matching their record. Numerically equal probabilities share a rank; alphabetical order within a tie is for display.

5. Revisit opponents each week

Each snapshot fits only the lines from games completed through the displayed week and cutoff. New market information can change the estimated difficulty of a September opponent. The September snapshot keeps its original information boundary. A bye can therefore change a team's current rank. Early rankings are especially sensitive to a single line. ESPN's Week 1 includes the opening weekend games commonly called Week 0. An in-progress week's date identifies the last included game.

What the polls tell you

AP and CFP ranks come from ESPN's dated archives. For a completed week we show the available post-week polls through the following Tuesday, capped at the season data cutoff. An in-progress week may carry the previous release. Each table lists both poll dates. The ranking calculation finishes before the poll data is joined. A blank CFP comparison early in the season means the committee had yet to release a poll.

How well does the model travel?

In the November 10 check, forecasts frozen before each week from Week 5 onward missed the final margin by an average of 12.58 points across 357 games. The actual market spread missed by 11.82 on those same games. Brier scores were 0.209 and 0.193 respectively, where lower is better. That gap measures some of the information lost in reducing a particular matchup to a pair of team ratings.

Changes in personnel, unusual travel, and matchup-specific advantages can all affect a spread. Repricing an early opponent with today's rating can also obscure a genuine change in that team's ability. Small differences between neighboring ranks deserve less weight than large differences in the underlying probabilities.
